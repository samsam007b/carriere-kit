# Career anti-patterns: advice to distrust, and why

> These anti-patterns target the AI assistant as much as the person using it. The default
> failure mode of a language model on this topic is to drift toward the dominant cluster in its
> training data: generic career coaching. This file is the counterweight, read before any
> advice with real stakes (an application, a negotiation, a pivot).

## PATTE-CAR-01: "follow your passion" / Ikigai as a decision tool

**Pattern**: starting from an Ikigai diagram, or a "what excites you," to settle a career
decision.

**Why it's wrong**: the popular Western Ikigai diagram (the love / good at / world needs / paid
for Venn diagram) is a popularization with no demonstrated empirical validity as a decision
tool. Cal Newport's *Career Capital Theory* argues that "follow your passion" is a poor
starting point: a sense of mission tends to emerge **after** rare skills accumulate, not
before.

**Instead**: frame every option in terms of **career capital** (what rare, hard-to-fake skills
or signals does this build?) and **optionality** (is it reversible, does it keep multiple doors
open?).

## PATTE-CAR-02: the degree as a sufficient signal

**Pattern**: leaning on a degree to carry credibility, or recommending waiting for one more
credential before acting.

**Why it's wrong**: signaling theory (Spence, 1973). In a market with asymmetric information, a
generic signal doesn't distinguish its holder from every other holder of the same signal (a
pooling equilibrium). What actually distinguishes a profile is a signal that's **costly to
fake**: shipped projects, checkable numbers, traceable results.

**Instead**: open every CV and pitch on the evidence. A degree supports institutional
credibility, it never leads.

## PATTE-CAR-03: flattening a hybrid profile into one label

**Pattern**: filing a dual-skill profile under whichever label reads easiest to a recruiter,
erasing the other half.

**Why it's wrong**: a hybrid profile's value sits precisely at the intersection, where
competition is thin. A language model, like a rushed recruiter, tends to pull an atypical
profile back toward the most frequent category in its training data. That's exactly the
behavior to resist.

**Instead**: state the hybridity as **one** capability, not two CVs stapled together. The
causal link between the two halves is the argument ("training in X gave me the reading of Y
systems, which I then applied on the Z side"), not a simple juxtaposition.

## PATTE-CAR-04: rounding up or inventing a figure

**Pattern**: rounding, extrapolating, or estimating a number for lack of a source at hand, "to
make it sound better."

**Why it's wrong**: a false figure a recruiter catches destroys the credibility of the entire
signal. A faked signal isn't a signal anymore, it's a liability (see PATTE-CAR-02).

**Instead**: if a figure isn't verified in `workspace/facts.md`, flag it as "to verify" and ask,
never estimate. An exact, modest figure beats an impressive, vague one.

## PATTE-CAR-05: generic statistics applied to the wrong market

**Pattern**: citing a national statistic from another country, or an aggregated continental
average, as if it applied unchanged to the local market.

**Why it's wrong**: large reports (from international bodies, consulting firms) are often
calibrated on a dominant market. Local markets carry their own dynamics: rules on combining
statuses, regional employment agencies, official shortage-occupation lists, seasonal hiring
cycles.

**Instead**: always cross-check a macro figure against its local counterpart before using it in
concrete advice. Official shortage-occupation lists are particularly instructive: a sector that
assumes it's scarce and doesn't appear on one has no automatic protection.

## PATTE-CAR-06: ignoring administrative deadlines

**Pattern**: giving strategic advice ("take your time," "explore") without mentioning the legal
windows that actually constrain the choice.

**Why it's wrong**: rights tied to entering the labor market (unemployment insertion benefits,
student status, regional aid, visas, regulated internship periods) carry age and timing
conditions assessed on a specific date, often **at the moment of filing**, not at the end of
studies. A right not claimed within the window is lost, with no appeal.

**Instead**: on any question of timing or status, check the administrative deadlines **before**
answering on strategy. On a point with real stakes, never settle for a web-sourced deduction;
get it confirmed by the competent authority or a legal helpline.

## PATTE-CAR-07: forcing a single objective

**Pattern**: pushing toward a binary choice, building a linear five-year plan with one single
outcome.

**Why it's wrong**: in a market with multiple buyers who value different forms of capital
differently (an auction market, as opposed to winner-take-all), fixing a single target too
early destroys optionality without guaranteeing anything. Running several leads in parallel,
including a mix of employment and a personal project, is a viable state, not a failure to
decide.

**Instead**: present a **portfolio** of options with their respective trade-offs, never a single
forced recommendation. Check the legal feasibility of combining statuses in the relevant
country while at it; it's often more permissive than assumed.

## The verification framework

Before delivering advice with real stakes, check:

1. **Sourced?** Every figure comes from `workspace/facts.md` or a verified source. Otherwise
   flag it "to verify" (PATTE-CAR-04).
2. **Positioning respected?** The advice doesn't flatten the profile into a single label
   (PATTE-CAR-03).
3. **Local?** Any macro data point is cross-checked against its equivalent in the target market
   (PATTE-CAR-05).
4. **Career capital, not passion?** The reasoning is built on rare skills and optionality
   (PATTE-CAR-01, 02).
5. **Administrative deadlines checked?** If the question touches on status, deadlines are
   explicitly mentioned (PATTE-CAR-06).
6. **Portfolio, not a single plan?** Multiple options stay open (PATTE-CAR-07).

**Go / no-go rule**: if 1 or 2 isn't checked, don't deliver the advice. Verify first. Points 3
to 6 are quality guardrails, applied by default, with an explicit note when one doesn't apply
to the context.
